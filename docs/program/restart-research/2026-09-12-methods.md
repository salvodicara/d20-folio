# From idea to complete product definition: proven methods, compared

Research brief for d20 Folio v2, 12 September 2026. Question: which well-known, proven methods take a
founder from "a vague idea in my head" to a complete definition of every object, feature, screen and
interaction *before* mockups and code — and how would an AI agent run them as a one-question-at-a-time
interview of a solo non-technical owner?

Method: web research over primary sources (official docs, the authors' own sites/books, GitHub
skill files) plus the best secondary summaries where the primary was paywalled. Every section lists
mechanics, artifacts, what the method is best at, its limits, and 2–4 sources. Section 11 gives the
recommended order for this owner.

Verification notes. Medium articles were read through a reader mirror (Cloudflare blocks direct
fetch). Lenny's PRD collection is paywalled; sections are quoted from Edo van Royen's public mirror.
No GDC talk specifically about BG3's *UI/UX* was found (Larian's GDC 2024 talks were on iteration,
cinematics and characters); the only public BG3 UX-process source is a consultant portfolio page
(Kat Craig) that could be read only through search snippets. Matt Pocock's `grill-me` SKILL.md body
was not fetchable (GitHub raw returns 404 for the current path; the aihero.dev docs were used
instead).

## 1. Amazon Working Backwards — PR/FAQ

**Mechanics.** Before any design or engineering, the owner of the idea writes a one-page mock press
release describing the product *as if it had already launched*, followed by an FAQ. The PR has a
fixed section order: heading (product name in one sentence) → subheading (target customer + benefit)
→ summary paragraph (city, date, one-paragraph overview) → problem paragraph (the customer's problem,
from the customer's side) → solution paragraph(s) (how the product solves it, what differentiates it)
→ one internal quote, one customer quote → "how to get started". The FAQ splits into an **external
FAQ** (what a customer would ask: price, how it works, how to get help, where to get it) and an
**internal FAQ** (what a skeptical executive would ask). The document is written alone in a few
hours, then iterated through widening circles of readers; each review is 15–20 minutes of silent
reading with inline comments, then 40 minutes of discussion. Amazon's stated purpose: force clarity of
thought around the customer experience before the team commits, and kill weak ideas cheaply.

**Internal FAQ question list** (from workingbackwards.com, Bryar & Carr):

- What do target customers use today? What problem does this solve? Why is it better/faster/cheaper?
- Who are the competitors? How big is the market? How many customers will actually pay/switch?
- Would customers reasonably change their behaviour for this?
- What are the hard problems (business, engineering, legal, UI)? What new capabilities are needed?
- Third-party dependencies? Legal/regulatory issues? Unit economics? Upfront investment? Time to
  profitability? Top three ways this fails?

**Reviewer criteria:** is the customer clearly defined; is the problem clearly defined; does the
solution address it; would customers change behaviour; on which dimension is it differentiated.

**Artifacts.** One-page press release; external FAQ; internal FAQ; (Amazon also attaches a rough
mock of the customer experience when useful).

**Best at.** Forcing the "why / for whom / what is different" decision in customer language; giving
an agent a compact seed document to interrogate; setting non-goals via the "would they switch"
question. The BMAD method has adopted it as its `bmad-prfaq` stress-test skill.

**Limits.** Says nothing about objects, screens or interactions; a PR/FAQ for an internal/hobby
product needs the market questions reframed ("would my table actually switch from D&D Beyond +
Owlbear?"). It is a gate, not a spec.

**Sources.**
- https://workingbackwards.com/resources/working-backwards-pr-faq/ (Bryar & Carr, structure + FAQ list)
- https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/
- https://productschool.com/blog/product-fundamentals/prfaq (template)

## 2. Basecamp Shape Up — shaping before design

**Mechanics.** Shaping is senior pre-work done *before* a project is scheduled and before any
designer or developer touches it. Four steps: (1) **Set boundaries** — fix the *appetite* (how much
time we are willing to spend: a "small batch" of 1–2 weeks or a "big batch" of 6 weeks) and narrow
the raw idea to a specific problem, refusing vague catch-all features ("the calendar is bad" → "I
need to see free days when scheduling"). (2) **Rough out the elements** at the right level of
abstraction with two tools. **Breadboarding** (from electronics prototyping): write the solution as
*Places* (screens/dialogs you can navigate to, underlined), *Affordances* (buttons, fields, copy the
user can act on, listed under the place) and *Connection lines* (which affordance leads to which
place). No layout, no visuals — it exposes functional questions such as "does enabling autopay pay
the current invoice?". **Fat-marker sketches** when spatial arrangement itself is the question:
drawn with a stroke so thick that detail is impossible. (3) **Address risks and rabbit holes**: walk
the solution slowly, find the hidden hard parts, declare *no-gos*. (4) **Write the pitch** with five
ingredients: Problem, Appetite, Solution, Rabbit holes, No-gos. Only then does the work go to a
betting table. Shape Up explicitly separates "wireframes are too concrete, words are too abstract"
and lands on breadboards as the middle.

**Artifacts.** Appetite statement; breadboards (places/affordances/connections); fat-marker
sketches; pitch (5 sections).

**Best at.** Fixing scope by time instead of by feature list; producing a screen/affordance
inventory *without* committing to layout; naming rabbit holes and no-gos explicitly — exactly the
"complete but not over-detailed" definition wanted before mockups.

**Limits.** Assumes a shaper with design and technical judgment (here: the agent must supply it and
the owner must confirm); appetite-driven scoping fights a "define everything" ambition — use
appetite per block, not for the whole product; breadboards are per-feature, not a whole-system
object model.

**Sources.**
- https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.3-chapter-04 (Find the Elements: breadboarding, fat marker)
- https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.5-chapter-06 (Write the Pitch: 5 ingredients)
- https://basecamp.com/shapeup (whole book, free)

## 3. Jobs-to-be-Done, Continuous Discovery (Torres) and The Mom Test

### 3a. JTBD — job stories and the switch interview

**Mechanics.** People "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. Intercom's framing, named
"Job Story" by Alan Klement: *"When ___ [situation], I want to ___ [motivation], so I can ___
[outcome]."* Example: "When an important new customer signs up, I want to be notified, so I can start
a conversation with them." Replacing "As a <persona>" with "When <situation>" removes assumed roles
and forces causality. Bob Moesta's **switch interview** reconstructs a real past switch along a
timeline — first thought → passive looking → active looking → trigger/decision → purchase → first use
— and codes it with the **four forces** (push of the current situation, pull of the new solution,
anxiety about the new, habit of the old). Sample questions: "What was the first moment you realised
the old way wasn't working?", "Walk me through what happened next — where were you, what else was
going on?", "What made you actually try something different?", "What worried you about the change?".

**Artifacts.** Job stories (one per situation); timeline + four-forces map for each real switch.

**Best at.** Turning "features I want" into situations at the table ("When a fight starts and five
players are looking at me…"); giving each feature a *why* the agent can test against.

**Limits.** Needs real past behaviour; the owner is the interviewee *and* a user, so the agent must
keep him on past sessions, not on imagined ones.

### 3b. Teresa Torres — Continuous Discovery Habits / Opportunity Solution Tree

**Mechanics.** Weekly customer interviews using **story-based questions** — "Tell me about the last
time you…" — never direct questions ("what do you want?"). Stories are synthesised into an
**experience map**, then into an **Opportunity Solution Tree**: root = desired *outcome* (a
measurable behaviour change), branches = *opportunities* (needs, pains, desires phrased from the
customer's side), leaves = *solutions* (several per opportunity), and under them *assumption tests*.
Rule of a good opportunity: "is there more than one way to address this?" — if only one, it is a
solution in disguise. Map after 3–4 interviews, revisit every 3–4 more. Trio (PM, design, eng)
decides together; here the trio is owner + agents.

**Artifacts.** Interview snapshots; experience map; opportunity solution tree.

**Best at.** Structuring the space *between* the vision and the feature list so that each feature
answers a named opportunity and competing solutions are compared, not assumed.

**Limits.** Designed for a continuous weekly rhythm with many customers; for a one-owner spec it
collapses to a single OST built from the owner's and his table's stories — still useful as the
feature-justification spine.

### 3c. The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick)

**Mechanics.** Three rules: (1) talk about *their life*, not your idea; (2) ask about *specifics in
the past*, not generics or opinions about the future; (3) talk less, listen more. Good questions:
"Talk me through the last time that happened", "What's hardest about that?", "How do you deal with
it today?", "What have you tried?", "Have you paid anything to solve it?". Bad questions: "Do you
think this is a good idea?", "Would you use X?", "Would you pay for X?", anything that pitches.
Three kinds of bad data to discard: compliments, fluff (generic claims, hypotheticals, future-tense
"I would"), and ideas (feature requests — dig for the motivation behind them instead). Prepare the
three most important questions before each conversation; be scared of at least one of them. A
conversation only counts if it ends in *commitment or advancement* (time, reputation or money).

**Artifacts.** Three-question prep list; notes tagged fact/compliment/fluff/idea; commitments log.

**Best at.** The *questioning discipline* the agent needs so it does not lead the witness: every
interview question in the recommended process is filtered through these three rules.

**Limits.** It is about validating a business, not enumerating features; the owner-as-customer
setting makes "commitment" moot — use rules 1–2 and the bad-data taxonomy only.

## 4. Object-Oriented UX (Sophia Prater) — the ORCA process

**Mechanics.** OOUX starts from the premise that people think in objects, so a digital system must
expose consistent, recognisable objects before any screen is drawn. ORCA (Objects, Relationships,
Calls-to-Action, Attributes) is Prater's 15-step protocol that sits as a "third diamond" (Structure)
between Research and Design. Four **rounds**, each visiting the four pillars:

- **Round 1 — Discovery ("smoke out complexity")**: *Noun foraging* — harvest nouns from research,
  the vision doc, competitor screens, the owner's speech; apply the *object litmus test* (has a
  definition, instances, attributes, value to user and business — a "verb" or a screen is not an
  object) and consolidate synonyms. *Nested-Object Matrix (NOM)* — an object-by-object grid whose
  cells state the relationship in both directions ("a CHARACTER has many ITEMS; an ITEM is owned by
  0..1 CHARACTER"). *CTA Matrix* — object-by-role grid whose cells list what each role can do to the
  object (create, view, edit, delete, plus domain verbs: equip, attune, transfer, roll, react). End
  with an *Object Map* — a colour-coded column per object: core content, metadata, nested objects,
  CTAs.
- **Round 2 — Requirements ("untangle complexity")**: *Object Guide* — "a glossary on steroids", one
  entry per object with definition, examples, non-examples, states. *MCSFD* for each relationship:
  Mechanics (how it is formed/broken), Cardinality, Sorting, Filtering, Dependencies. Expand the CTA
  matrix into *object-oriented user stories* ("As a DM, I can *transfer* an ITEM to a CHARACTER").
  For every attribute: type, allowed values, required/optional, conditional logic, who can edit.
- **Round 3 — Prioritisation**: downgrade/eliminate/offload/combine objects; draw the *Nav Flow*
  (how one gets from object to related object); phase CTAs and decide CTA placement; force-rank
  attributes to produce the prioritised Object Map.
- **Round 4 — Representation**: sketch each object's *card, detail, list and landing* views; stitch
  into a navigable prototype; test with the four validation questions (right objects? relationships
  make sense? CTAs relevant/findable? attributes complete?). "Then and only then" do user flows and
  interaction design begin.

Prater names four failure modes ORCA prevents: masked objects, isolated objects, broken objects
(CTAs in the wrong place), arbitrary shape-shifting (inconsistent variants of the same object).

**Artifacts.** Noun list → object list; Nested-Object Matrix; CTA Matrix; Object Map; Object Guide;
MCSFD table; OO user stories; Nav Flow; card/detail/list/landing sketches.

**Best at.** Exactly the requested inventory: *every object, every attribute, every relationship,
every call-to-action*, in a form an agent can maintain as tables and a non-technical owner can read.
It maps one-to-one onto d20 Folio's own rule "a shared fact has one model home" and onto the
Character schema. An agent facilitator already exists (s1dd4rth/ooux-skill: Quick 20-min / Working
60-min / Full multi-session ORCA).

**Limits.** Weak on *behaviour over time* (turn order, reactions, resolution) — that is Event
Storming's job; needs research input ("put good research in one end"); Round 2 is heavy and can be
partly deferred; the full course material is commercial (ORCA Handbook, Launch Guide behind newsletter
sign-up).

**Sources.**
- https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/introducing-orca-the-third-diamond-in-your-ux-process-23a1babb0389 (Prater, primary)
- https://ooux.com/offerings/fourfails (prioritisation substeps, four failures) ; https://ooux.com/start
- https://alistapart.com/article/object-oriented-ux/ (Prater's original A List Apart piece: object mapping)
- https://github.com/s1dd4rth/ooux-skill (agent skill that facilitates ORCA)

## 5. User Story Mapping (Jeff Patton) and Event Storming (Alberto Brandolini)

### 5a. User Story Mapping

**Mechanics.** Build a two-dimensional map of the user's journey. Across the top, the **backbone**:
user *activities* in narrative left-to-right order ("prepare session → run encounter → resolve turn →
hand out loot → end session"), each broken into *steps/tasks*. Under each step, the stories that
fulfil it, ordered top-to-bottom by priority. Patton's workshop: *frame* the product (who, why,
outcomes); *map the big picture* by telling the story of a day of use, backbone first, breadth before
depth; *explore* — for each step ask "what could go wrong, what else could they do, what would make it
great" and add stories; *slice* horizontally into releases; the first slice is the **walking
skeleton** — the smallest end-to-end path that lets a real user complete the whole journey. The map
stays on the wall (or in a doc) as the shared model; it beats a flat backlog because it shows gaps
and keeps every story in the context of a step.

**Artifacts.** Story map (backbone → steps → stories); release slices; walking skeleton.

**Best at.** Ordering everything found by ORCA and Event Storming into the *narrative of a session*;
defining "one whole session without Owlbear, D&D Beyond or a calculator" as a walking skeleton.

**Limits.** Stories stay at "what", not "how the screen behaves"; without an object model the map
tends to duplicate concepts under different names.

### 5b. Event Storming

**Mechanics.** A workshop invented by Brandolini (DDD community) to discover a domain by its
*events*. **Big Picture** phases: kick-off → *chaotic exploration* (everyone writes past-tense
domain events on orange stickies: "Initiative rolled", "Reaction triggered", "Item transferred") →
*enforce the timeline* → add *people* (yellow) and *external systems* (pink) → *explicit walkthrough*
(narrate the timeline start-to-end) → *reverse narrative* (walk it backwards to find missing events)
→ *problems & opportunities* (magenta hotspots, green opportunities) → arrow voting. **Process
Modelling** adds the grammar *Event → Policy (purple, "whenever X then Y") → Command (blue, verb +
noun, issued by a person reading a Read Model (green)) → System → Event*. **Software Design** adds
aggregates (yellow: the thing that accepts commands and emits events), UI stickies and bounded
contexts. The "picture that explains everything" is that grammar laid out in swimlanes.

**Artifacts.** Event timeline; people/systems; hotspots; policies; commands; read models;
aggregates; bounded contexts.

**Best at.** Discovering *behaviour and rules*: the combat automation seam — every rule of 5e turns
into a policy ("whenever a creature leaves reach, then offer Opportunity Attack"), every UI action
into a command, every consequence into an event. It maps directly onto d20 Folio's append-only
Encounter log (events) and reducer (aggregates), so the spec and the architecture share vocabulary.

**Limits.** Designed for a room of experts; solo it becomes the agent proposing events and the
owner correcting — workable, and Brandolini's own site says online/solo is a poor fit for *Big
Picture*, less so for Process Modelling. Not a screen method.

**Sources.**
- Patton, *User Story Mapping* (O'Reilly 2014); https://www.jpattonassociates.com/story-mapping/
- https://marcabraham.com/2012/07/27/jeff-pattons-story-mapping/ (backbone, walking skeleton)
- https://www.eventstorming.com/resources/ (Brandolini, formats) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_storming

## 6. Google Ventures Design Sprint (Knapp)

**Mechanics.** Five days, one team, one *Decider*. **Monday**: start at the end — write the
*long-term goal* ("in a year, our table…"); list *sprint questions* (turn each "how could this fail?"
into "can we…?"); draw the *map* (actors on the left, the goal on the right, 5–15 steps between);
*ask the experts* 15–30 minutes each while everyone writes *How-Might-We* notes; cluster and vote;
*pick a target* — one customer and one moment on the map. **Tuesday**: *lightning demos* — 3-minute
tours of existing solutions (competitors and analogies), capturing the good idea of each in a
sketch; *four-step sketch* (notes 20 min → ideas 20 min → Crazy 8s → one three-panel solution
sketch). **Wednesday**: *art museum*, *heat map* dots, *speed critique*, *straw poll*, Decider
*supervote*; then a 15-panel *storyboard*. **Thursday**: prototype only the storyboard ("fake it",
realistic surface, 7 hours). **Friday**: five one-on-one user tests, five-act interview, findings on
a grid.

**Artifacts.** Long-term goal; sprint questions; map; HMW clusters; target; lightning-demo notes;
solution sketches; storyboard; prototype; test scorecard.

**Best at (for one owner + agents).** Monday's kit — long-term goal, sprint questions, map, target —
is the best-known compact "frame the problem" ritual; *lightning demos* is the formal name for what
CLAUDE.md calls "giants' shoulders" (BG3, D&D Beyond, Foundry, Roll20 tours); the 15-panel
*storyboard* is a proven way to define one flow end-to-end before pixels.

**Limits.** Built for divergence among 7 people and for validating one risky bet in a week, not for
defining a whole product; sketch/vote/supervote collapse when there is one human — use the agent to
generate alternatives and the owner as Decider; Friday tests need real players from the group.

**Sources.**
- https://www.character.vc/guide/design-sprint (Knapp's official step-by-step guide; thesprintbook.com redirects here)
- https://www.gv.com/sprint/
- https://laura-eiche.medium.com/6-tips-on-how-to-run-your-first-solo-google-design-sprint-afa1dd75009 (solo adaptation)

## 7. Game industry: GDDs, feature/UX specs, Hodent's framework, BG3/Larian, UX teardowns

**GDD practice.** Modern studios treat the GDD as a living wiki, not a 200-page tome: start with a
one-pager (elevator pitch, design pillars, non-goals), then a core loop written as a chain ("explore
→ fight → loot → upgrade"), then one document per *system* (mechanics, progression, economy, combat,
edge cases), one per *content* class with explicit volumes, a *UX/UI* section (wireframes, UI flows,
HUD requirements, accessibility) and production/risks. Single-feature detail lives in separate
*feature specs* linked from the GDD; changelogs track decisions. Nuclino/GitBook templates and
Scott Rogers' *Level Up!* template chapter are the commonly copied outlines.

**Game UX flows (studio practice).** Game UX designers document architecture with flow diagrams
before prototypes because "games are sequential: screens appear one at a time and information and
interactions depend on context". Joss Querné's taxonomy (used at Ubisoft-style studios): *Navigation
Flow* (screens/overlays and their entries/exits), *Information Architecture Flow* (which information
is present on each screen at each moment), *Interaction Flow* (player actions vs system states), and
*Wire Flow* (wireframes as a diagram). Scoped High (whole game) / Mid (a system) / Low (a feature).
Rules: never cross similar lines, never duplicate a screen, join lines. This is the games form of
the "screen and state inventory".

**Celia Hodent — The Gamer's Brain framework.** Game UX = *usability* + *engage-ability*. Seven
usability pillars, each a checklist question: **Signs & feedback** (does the game say what you can do
and what happened?), **Clarity** (are they perceived?), **Form follows function** (can you infer
function from look?), **Consistency** (across the game and the genre), **Minimum workload**
(cognitive, memory, physical load where challenge is not intended), **Error prevention & recovery**,
**Flexibility & accessibility**. Engage-ability = **Motivation** (competence, autonomy, relatedness,
meaningful goals/rewards), **Emotion** (game feel, sensory design, discovery), **Game flow**
(difficulty pacing, learning curve/onboarding). Used at Epic as a per-feature UX review checklist.

**Larian / BG3.** Vincke's GDC 2024 talk covered iteration: early builds "almost unrecognisable",
the pipeline reorganised "for iteration and flexibility", Early Access (Aug 2020 → Aug 2023) as the
feedback loop. On UI specifically, the public record is thin: the game's hotbar/radial/inventory
underwent large EA revisions driven by player feedback (Larian forum threads "Let's talk new UI"),
and a UX consultant's portfolio (Kat Craig) describes work on the keyboard/mouse vs gamepad
disconnect and inventory/combat flows. No dedicated BG3 UI/UX GDC talk was found. Practical
take-away: treat BG3's *shipped* UI as the reference to inventory (Game UI Database catalogues its
screens), not its process.

**Competitive UX evaluation in games** (Cerise G., games UX designer): (1) write a *goal* that
names the design task and audience; (2) choose 3–5 competitor titles (household names, same
platform/perspective, similar store tags); (3) *write the evaluation template before playing* —
work backwards from the goal to the questions per title (e.g. control-scheme overview, cost of a
mistake, steps to switch tool); (4) play and *record* (OBS), think aloud, timestamp key moments,
fill the template afterwards; (5) summarise: does each title solve it, pros/cons, what is consistent
across all titles (conformity vs innovation). PlaytestCloud adds competitor playtests (30-minute
sessions, 5 players) when needed.

**Artifacts.** One-page GDD; per-system docs; feature specs; navigation / IA / interaction / wire
flows; Hodent checklist per feature; competitor evaluation template + summary.

**Best at.** The flow taxonomy and the Hodent checklist are the game-native way to define screens,
states and interactions and to review them; the competitor template is the disciplined "BG3 does X,
D&D Beyond does Y" dossier the repo already mandates (rule 30).

**Limits.** GDD templates are genre-generic and prose-heavy; Hodent is a *review* lens, not a
generator; BG3 process evidence is second-hand.

## 8. Competitive teardown / UX benchmarking methods

**Heuristic evaluation (Nielsen).** 3–5 evaluators independently walk the interface against ten
heuristics — visibility of system status; match with the real world; user control and freedom;
consistency and standards; error prevention; recognition rather than recall; flexibility and
efficiency; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors;
help and documentation — and log each finding as (screen, heuristic violated, severity 0–4,
evidence). Single evaluator finds ~35 % of problems, five find ~75 %. Template columns: *ID · screen
/ state · task step · heuristic · description · severity · screenshot · recommendation*.

**Hierarchical Task Analysis.** Decompose a goal into numbered sub-goals (1, 1.1, 1.2…) and
actions, with *plans* stating the order/conditions ("do 1.1 then 1.2; if X, 1.3"). Built from
observation for existing products and from interviews for new ones. Used to compare two products'
step counts for the same task and to spot inefficiency ("resolve one attack in BG3 = 3 clicks; in D&D
Beyond = 9 clicks + mental arithmetic").

**Feature / interaction / state inventories.** *Feature comparison matrix*: features down, products
across, cells = absent / basic / good / excellent. *Interaction inventory*: for each screen, every
affordance and what it does (this is the breadboard of section 2 applied to a competitor).
*State inventory*: per component, the list of its states and the transitions between them; UXmatters'
notation writes `[Component: State]` in the flow and distinguishes synchronous vs asynchronous
changes. *Task-flow analysis*: side-by-side screens per step with friction points marked.

**Wireflows (NN/g).** Wireframe of each step + arrows from the hotspot that triggers the transition
+ the result (a new screen *or the same screen with changed content*). Preferred over flowcharts
for apps with few pages whose content changes dynamically — the character sheet and the combat
tracker are exactly that.

**UXPin's six-step process.** Define the objective → pick 4–6 competitors (direct + indirect) →
fix evaluation criteria → run 2–3 methods (heuristics, feature matrix, task-flow, benchmarking,
review mining, comparative testing) → structured report → backlog items.

**Artifacts.** Heuristic findings table; HTA tree; feature matrix; per-screen interaction inventory;
per-component state inventory; wireflows; task-flow comparison.

**Best at.** Making the reference dossier systematic and screen-by-screen; producing the "state
inventory" that mockups otherwise leave implicit (hover, disabled, loading, empty, error, hidden-DM).

**Limits.** Evaluators must actually use the product (screenshots/recordings needed); heuristics
find usability faults, not missing features — pair with the feature matrix.

## 9. Requirement formats agents build from well

**EARS (Mavin, Rolls-Royce, RE'09).** Controlled natural language with six patterns and a fixed
clause order (preconditions → trigger → system → response):
- Ubiquitous: `The <system> shall <response>.`
- Event-driven: `WHEN <trigger>, the <system> shall <response>.`
- State-driven: `WHILE <state>, the <system> shall <response>.`
- Unwanted behaviour: `IF <undesired condition>, THEN the <system> shall <response>.`
- Optional feature: `WHERE <feature is included>, the <system> shall <response>.`
- Complex: `WHILE <state>, WHEN <trigger>, the <system> shall <response>.`
Prevents ambiguity, vagueness, omission, duplication, wordiness and untestability; tool-checkable.

**Gherkin / Given-When-Then.** `Given <context> When <action> Then <observable outcome>`, one
scenario per path (happy path, each error, each edge), behaviour not implementation. Automatable as
acceptance tests; AutomationPanda's `gherkin-guidelines.md` is written specifically to be fed to
coding agents.

**Kiro spec format (AWS).** `.kiro/specs/<feature>/requirements.md` — numbered *Requirement N*,
each with a user story and numbered EARS acceptance criteria ("WHEN … THE SYSTEM SHALL …", plus
"SHALL CONTINUE TO …" for preservation); `design.md` — Overview, Architecture, Components, Data
models, Error handling, Testing strategy (with sequence diagrams); `tasks.md` — checkbox tasks each
referencing requirement IDs. Approval gate after each file; "Requirements-first" or "Design-first"
entry. Tasks run in dependency "waves".

**PRD templates (Lenny's collection, via van Royen).** Common spine across Lenny's 1-pager, Kevin
Yien/Square, Intercom, Asana, Figma: *problem statement first and separated from the solution*;
goals and non-goals; job stories (Intercom, must fit an A4); staged reviews (Square: Draft → Problem
review → Solution review → Launch review); Asana's two-stage Brief → Proposal with a "Non-goals"
section. Matt Pocock's `to-spec` template (Problem statement, Solution, a LONG numbered list of user
stories, Implementation decisions, Testing decisions, Out of scope, Further notes) is the agent-era
minimal PRD. Codex's proposed `/specs/*.md` uses Overview / Requirements with RFC 2119 verbs / Design.

**Artifacts.** requirements.md (EARS), feature files (Gherkin), design.md, tasks.md, PRD/1-pager.

**Best at.** EARS for system rules and automation policies (every 5e rule becomes one line an agent
can test); Gherkin for user-visible flows and undo; Kiro's three-file split for traceability from
requirement → design → task.

**Limits.** EARS/Gherkin describe behaviour, not structure — they come *after* the object model and
flows; PRDs are strategic and short, not exhaustive.

## 10. AI-agent-led "interview the founder" processes

**Claude Code interview mode (Thariq / VelvetShark / Developers Digest, 2025–26).** Prompt: *"Read
@SPEC and interview me in detail using the AskUserQuestion tool about literally anything
[technical implementation, UI/UX, trade-offs]. Make sure questions aren't obvious. Be very in-depth
and continue until it's complete. Then write the spec to the file."* Observed behaviour: 30–40+
questions, multiple-choice where possible, covering scope, data model, edge cases (stale/duplicate
data), output formats, explicit exclusions, acceptance criteria; stops when "requirements are
locked"; output is a `spec.md` handed to a *separate* implementation session. Order emerges from the
seed spec, not from a fixed script.

**Matt Pocock — grilling / grill-me / grill-with-docs / to-spec.** Core prompt: *"Interview me
relentlessly about every aspect of this plan until we reach a shared understanding. Walk down each
branch of the design tree resolving dependencies between decisions one by one. If a question can be
answered by exploring the codebase, explore the codebase instead. For each question, provide your
recommended answer."* Mechanics: model the design as a *tree of decisions*; the *frontier* is the set
of questions whose prerequisites are all settled; ask a *round* = the whole frontier (numbered, each
with a `➡️` recommended default the user can accept by number), never two questions in one round
where one depends on the other; answering reveals the next frontier; typical sessions 3–4 rounds,
13–50 questions; stop when the frontier is empty, then a *confirmation gate*. Config option "ask one
question at a time". When a question is *ungrillable* ("how should this feel?") stop and build a
throwaway prototype. `grill-with-docs` runs the same interview against a codebase and, via the
`domain-modeling` skill, writes `CONTEXT.md` (a glossary only — "sharpen fuzzy language", "challenge
against the glossary", "stress-test with concrete scenarios") and ADRs (only when hard to reverse,
surprising, and a real trade-off). `to-spec` then synthesises the conversation *without* another
interview into the PRD template of section 9.

**Codex.** No shipped interview mode; the maintainers' discussion (#7355) leans to "ask up to four
clarifying questions when trade-offs exist" for Plans and persistent `/specs/*.md` (Overview,
Requirements with RFC 2119 verbs, Design) for Specs; community prompts add "do not write code yet;
ask your highest-value questions first in small batches about scope, edge cases, data shapes and how
we will know it works; cap the interview; then write SPEC.md".

**BMAD Method.** Named agents: Analyst (Mary), PM (John), Architect (Winston), UX Designer (Sally),
Developer (Amelia). Planning chain: `bmad-brainstorming` (facilitator / creative-partner / ideate
stances; output `brainstorm-intent.md`) → `bmad-forge-idea` (pressure-tests a half-formed idea:
"pins down vague terminology, questions the central claim first, drives unresolved branches to
concrete decisions, proposes specific answers"; can attack/defend/switch roles; output
`forged-idea.md`) → `bmad-product-brief` (who is the customer, what problem, what does success look
like) or `bmad-prfaq` (Amazon method) → `bmad-prd` built *section by section* with the user (goals &
constraints, functional and non-functional requirements, epics/stories) → `bmad-ux` (`DESIGN.md`,
`EXPERIENCE.md`) → `bmad-architecture`. At decision points *advanced elicitation* offers critique
methods to run on what was just generated: pre-mortem, first principles, inversion, red team vs blue
team, Socratic questioning, constraint removal, stakeholder mapping, analogical reasoning.

**OOUX agent skill (s1dd4rth/ooux-skill).** Facilitates ORCA step by step (noun foraging → litmus
test → consolidation → NOM → MCSFD → role mapping → CTA matrix → attributes → object map) with
three depths (Quick 20 min, Working 60 min, Full).

**What they ask, in what order (synthesis of the observed scripts).** (1) purpose/outcome and
who; (2) scope boundaries and non-goals; (3) the domain vocabulary and the things in it;
(4) main flows and their edge cases; (5) data shapes and persistence; (6) trade-offs with a
recommended default; (7) "how will we know it works" — acceptance criteria; (8) confirmation gate,
then synthesis into a spec.

**Best at.** Pocock's frontier/round mechanics are the best-documented *questioning algorithm*; BMAD
supplies the *phase sequence* and elicitation critiques; Claude's AskUserQuestion supplies the
multiple-choice UX the owner asked for ("one question at a time" is a supported switch).

**Limits.** All are generic: none knows that objects come before flows or that rules need events.
Their question order must be seeded by the domain methods above. `grill-me` is stateless; only
`grill-with-docs` leaves a paper trail.

## 11. Synthesis — recommended order for this owner

Situation: one non-technical owner who is also a DM and a player; an agent that interviews him one
question at a time (AskUserQuestion, Pocock's frontier rule, Mom Test question discipline); two
shipped references to copy (BG3 for the table, D&D Beyond for the character); an existing v2
program, PRODUCT.md and Character schema that the spec must reconcile with, not replace.

Principle borrowed from Prater: *structure before screens* — the "third diamond". Principle
borrowed from Shape Up: *breadboards before wireframes*. Principle borrowed from EARS/Gherkin:
*every behaviour as a testable sentence*. Principle borrowed from Pocock: *walk the decision tree,
ask only the frontier, propose a default, stop when nothing is silently assumed*.

**How the interview runs across the phases.**

- Each phase is a sub-tree. The agent asks only the *frontier* (questions whose prerequisites are
  answered), one question at a time (the supported "ask one question at a time" switch), with a
  recommended default drawn from the reference dossier ("BG3 does X; D&D Beyond does Y; I recommend
  X because…"), and multiple-choice options where AskUserQuestion allows.
- Every question passes the Mom Test filter: about the owner's *past sessions* and the table's
  *life*, not about whether he likes an idea; feature requests are logged as ideas and followed by
  "tell me about the last time you needed that".
- Phases 3 and 4 are where the "dream house" is enumerated: an object is not done until it has a
  definition, instances, attributes with types and editors, relationships with cardinality and
  sort/filter, and a CTA per role; a rule is not done until it is a policy line with trigger,
  command and event.
- Ungrillable questions ("how should the hotbar feel?") are parked for a prototype (Pocock) or a
  fat-marker sketch by Astra, per the owner/decision split in CLAUDE.md.
- The gate at phase 10 is the explicit shared-understanding confirmation; the resulting documents
  are the spec Astra mocks from and Claude builds from, with `requirements.md` criteria becoming the
  tests.

**Why this order and not another.** Frame (0) exists so that every later "why" has one sentence to
point at. Stories (1) and dossier (2) exist because ORCA's own author says the process needs "good
research in one end" and rule 30 needs the real reference beside our rendition. Objects (3) come
before events (4) because every event is "something happened *to an object*", and both come before
features (5) because a feature is a set of CTAs, policies and views over already-named things —
which is what removes the "vague idea" without inventing anything. Journey (6) orders those features
into a session; screens (7) and states (8) are then a *representation* of decided structure, exactly
where Prater and Shape Up put them; acceptance criteria (9) are written last because EARS and Gherkin
need a named system, named states and named triggers to be unambiguous.
